Chapter 01

Descriptive Statistics

Five students read 2, 3, 3, 4, and 13 pages last night. What is a fair way to describe what the group read? The answer depends on what you mean by “typical.”

Find the center

The mean is what everyone would have if you shared the total evenly. Add the pages to get 25, then divide by five to get 5 pages. The median is the middle value after sorting, which is 3 pages. With an even number of values, average the middle two. The mode is the most common value, also 3 pages here.

Look at the spread and shape

The range is 13 − 2 = 11 pages. The interquartile range measures the width of the middle half, from the first quartile to the third quartile. Standard deviation measures spread around the mean in the original units. A dot plot shows individual values. A histogram groups values into intervals. Here the dots stretch farther to the right, so the data are right-skewed. The median gives a better sense of what most students read in this example.

Try it

If the last student had read 23 pages instead of 13, what would happen to the mean and median?

Show answer

The mean would rise to 7 pages because the total rises to 35. The median would stay at 3 because the middle entry stays in place.

Go deeper with the math

The mean is the sum divided by the number of observations. The sample variance averages squared deviations using a divisor of n − 1, and the sample standard deviation is its square root. Here xi is one value and n is the number of values. Kurtosis reflects the tendency toward extreme values relative to a normal distribution.

$$\bar{x}=\frac{\sum_i x_i}{n},\quad s^2=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2}{n-1},\quad s=\sqrt{s^2}$$

Chapter 02

Probability Basics

A bag has three blue marbles and two orange ones. You shake the bag and pick a marble without looking. How likely is it to be blue?

Count the possible outcomes

Each of the five marbles is equally likely to be picked, and three are blue. The probability of blue is 3 out of 5, or 60%. An outcome is one possible result. An event is a set of results you care about, such as drawing any blue marble. A probability of 0 means impossible, and 1 means certain. A long run of repeated draws with replacement should produce a blue fraction close to 60%.

Ask what changed

If you draw a blue marble and keep it, two of the four remaining marbles are blue. The chance of a second blue is now 2/4. This is a conditional probability, a chance calculated with new information. If you put the first marble back and mix again, the chance stays 3/5. In that setup the two draws are independent, meaning the first result leaves the second draw's chances the same.

Try it

What is the chance of drawing two blue marbles in a row if you keep the first marble out?

Show answer

The first blue has probability 3/5. Given that result, the second blue has probability 2/4. Multiply to get 6/20, or 30%.

Go deeper with the math

The complement of event A includes every outcome outside A. In the multiplication rule, the vertical bar means “given.” For an “A or B” question, subtract any overlap counted twice.

$$P(\text{not }A)=1-P(A),\quad P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)$$

Chapter 03

Random Variables & Distributions

Flip two fair coins and count the heads. Your count could be 0, 1, or 2. Which count should appear most often?

Turn an outcome into a number

A random variable gives a number to each outcome. The outcomes here are TT, TH, HT, and HH, where T means tails and H means heads. They are equally likely if the flips are independent. TT gives zero heads, TH and HT each give one, and HH gives two. A probability distribution shows the chances for the different values. One head has probability 2/4, twice the chance of zero or two heads.

Counts and measurements

A discrete variable has countable values, like the number of heads. The binomial distribution describes a success count from a fixed number of independent tries with the same success chance. A continuous variable can be modeled on a number line, like the exact time for a race. We talk about the chance of finishing within an interval, such as 12 to 13 seconds. One continuous model is the normal distribution, a symmetric bell-shaped curve where area represents probability.

Try it

If you play the two-coin game many times, how many heads per game would you expect on average?

Show answer

About 1 head per game. Multiply each possible count by its probability and add: 0 × 1/4 + 1 × 1/2 + 2 × 1/4 = 1.

Go deeper with the math

The expected value of a discrete variable is the probability-weighted average. Here X is the random variable and x is one possible value.

$$E(X)=\sum_x x\,P(X=x)$$

Chapter 04

Sampling Distributions

Suppose you want to know how long students at your school spend getting there. You randomly pick 25 students and find their average travel time. Another group of 25 will probably give a different average.

Imagine doing it again and again

The whole school is the population. The students you pick are a sample. A statistic is a number calculated from a sample, like its mean. Picture taking many samples of the same size with the same method, then plotting one mean for each sample. The pattern of those means is the sampling distribution. Each dot now stands for a whole sample.

Bigger samples usually give steadier averages

The standard error tells us how much a statistic varies from sample to sample. For independent draws from the same population, sample means get steadier as sample size grows. The central limit theorem explains why the pattern of means tends to look bell-shaped as samples get large, when the population has finite variance. Individual travel times can still have a long right tail.

Try it

If the standard error with 25 students is 2 minutes, what would you expect with 100 independent observations from the same population?

Show answer

About 1 minute. The sample size is four times as large, so the standard error is divided by the square root of four, which is 2.

Go deeper with the math

For independent observations from one population, divide the population standard deviation σ by the square root of sample size n. In practice, the sample standard deviation s can estimate σ.

$$\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}$$

Chapter 05

Estimation & Confidence Intervals

Your sample says students take 20 minutes to get to school on average. How precise is that guess about the whole school?

Give a best guess and a range

A parameter is a number about a whole population. The school’s true average travel time is a parameter. A point estimate is a single best guess from the sample, here 20 minutes. If the margin of error is 3 minutes, the confidence interval runs from 17 to 23 minutes. A narrower interval gives a more precise estimate when the method and design are appropriate.

What 95% confidence means

Imagine collecting many new random samples and making an interval from each. If the method’s conditions hold, about 95 out of 100 intervals made this way will cover the true mean over the long run. The true mean stays fixed while the intervals move. A larger sample usually shrinks the margin of error. A higher confidence level, such as 99%, usually makes the interval wider for the same data. A survey that leaves out certain students can still give a biased estimate.

Try it

A survey estimates 24 minutes with a margin of error of 4 minutes. What interval would you report?

Show answer

20 to 28 minutes. Subtract 4 to get the lower end and add 4 to get the upper end.

Go deeper with the math

For a one-sample t interval, multiply the estimated standard error by a critical value t*, which depends on the confidence level and degrees of freedom. The usual interval assumes independent observations and works best with a roughly normal population for small samples.

$$\bar{x}\pm t^*\frac{s}{\sqrt n}$$

Chapter 06

Hypothesis Testing

Why this method? You have a claim (null hypothesis) and want to see whether your data are surprising enough under that claim to question it.

How to run it: Depends on the test (t-test, chi-square, etc.). The logic is the same: compare your result to what the null predicts.

What the result means: The p-value is the probability of a result at least this extreme if the null were true. A small p-value is evidence against the null, not proof that your alternative is correct. Always pair it with an estimate and interval.

A game coin lands heads 9 times in 10 flips. Would a fair coin do that often? Let’s compare what happened with what fairness predicts.

Start with a claim you can test

The null hypothesis is the starting claim, here that heads has probability 1/2. The alternative hypothesis here says the chance differs from 1/2 in either direction. That makes this a two-sided test. Assuming a fair coin and independent flips, results as far from five heads as our result include 0, 1, 9, or 10 heads. Those outcomes account for 22 of the 1,024 equally likely flip sequences.

Decide how unusual is unusual enough

The p-value is the chance of a result at least this extreme if the null and the test assumptions hold. Here it is 22/1,024, about 0.0215. If you chose a cutoff of 0.05 in advance, you would reject the fairness claim at that cutoff. If the p-value were larger than the cutoff, the sample would give too little evidence to reject. A false alarm is called a Type I error. Missing a real difference is called a Type II error.

Try it

Suppose you chose a stricter cutoff of 0.01 before flipping. What would you conclude with the same p-value?

Show answer

You would fail to reject the fairness claim because 0.0215 is above 0.01. More flips could help you learn whether the pattern holds up.

Go deeper with the math

The significance level α limits the long-run false alarm rate when the null holds and the test is valid. The p-value describes how surprising the data are under the starting claim and model. Use an estimate and interval to discuss how big a difference might be.

$$H_0:p_{\mathrm{heads}}=0.5,\quad H_A:p_{\mathrm{heads}}\ne 0.5$$

Chapter 07

Comparing Two Groups (t-tests)

Why this method? You have two groups (or paired measurements) and want to know if their means differ more than you’d expect from ordinary variation.

How to run it (R): t.test(score ~ group, data = df)  ·  Python: from scipy import stats; stats.ttest_ind(a, b)

What the result means: A small p-value suggests the observed difference is unlikely if the true means were equal. Also look at the confidence interval and effect size (Cohen’s d) to judge practical importance.

A teacher wants to compare two ways to practice spelling. One group can use flashcards and another can write words by hand. How can the teacher tell whether a score difference is large compared with the ordinary ups and downs in scores?

Find out who goes with whom

An independent-samples t-test compares the means of two separate groups, such as different students assigned to flashcards or writing. The Welch t-test can handle different amounts of spread in the two groups. A paired t-test starts with pairs, such as each student’s score before and after a practice week. Find each person’s change first, then test whether the average change differs from zero.

Compare the difference with its uncertainty

Imagine the flashcard group averages 82 and the writing group averages 76. The estimated difference is 6 points. If its standard error is 3 points, the difference is two standard errors above zero. The test uses a t distribution to judge how unusual this is under a zero difference. Look at the confidence interval too. For a fair cause-and-effect comparison, randomly assign students to methods. An existing classroom difference could reflect something else, such as prior skill.

Try it

Mia scores 70 before practice and 76 after. Jay scores 80 before and 82 after. What two numbers go into a paired analysis?

Show answer

The changes are +6 and +2 points, with a mean change of +4. Each change belongs to one student.

Go deeper with the math

A t statistic divides the distance from the null value by the estimated standard error. Use independent observations across groups for Welch, or independent pairs for a paired test. With small samples, check for strong skew and unusual points in the groups or in the paired differences, respectively.

$$t=\frac{\text{estimated difference}-\text{null difference}}{\operatorname{SE}(\text{estimated difference})}$$

Chapter 08

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

Why this method? You have three or more groups and want to test whether their means are all the same, without running many separate t-tests.

How to run it (R): aov(distance ~ design, data = df)  ·  Python: from scipy import stats; stats.f_oneway(g1, g2, g3)

What the result means: A significant F-test says at least one group mean differs. It does not tell you which groups differ — follow up with pairwise comparisons or contrasts, and report effect size (η² or ω²).

A paper-airplane club tests three designs. Each design has several flights. Can one test check whether the designs all have the same mean flight distance?

Compare two kinds of variation

In one-way ANOVA, one factor, airplane design, splits the flights into groups. Compare how far apart the group means are with how much individual distances vary inside each group. If group means are far apart and the flights in each group cluster tightly, the evidence for a difference is stronger. The F statistic divides the variation between groups by the variation within groups, after adjusting each for how many values can vary freely. For the flights in the picture, those adjusted amounts are 27 and 1, so F = 27.

Find out what a result tells you

The null claim is that all the population means are equal. A small p-value supports the idea that at least one mean differs. To find which pairs differ, use follow-up comparisons that account for testing several pairs, such as Tukey’s method after a standard one-way ANOVA. The standard test assumes independent observations, roughly normal errors within groups, and similar group variances. Repeated flights from the same plane or pilot can call for a design or analysis that handles that dependence.

Try it

Imagine the group means are still 4, 7, and 10 meters, but flights within each group are now much more spread out. What happens to F?

Show answer

F gets smaller if the group sizes and means stay fixed. The within-group variation in the denominator gets larger.

Go deeper with the math

A mean square (MS) is a sum of squared deviations divided by its degrees of freedom. With k groups and N observations, the between-group degrees of freedom are k − 1 and the within-group degrees of freedom are N − k.

$$F=\frac{MS_{\mathrm{between}}}{MS_{\mathrm{within}}}$$

Chapter 09

Correlation & Simple Linear Regression

Why this method? You want to describe the straight-line relationship between two continuous variables (correlation) or predict one from the other (regression).

How to run it (R): cor.test(x, y) or lm(y ~ x, data = df)  ·  Python: scipy.stats.pearsonr(x, y) or statsmodels.OLS

What the result means: Correlation (r) shows direction and strength (−1 to +1). In regression, the slope is the expected change in y for a one-unit increase in x. Always check the scatterplot — a strong curve can still give a weak r.

Do students who practice more free throws tend to make more baskets? Give each player one dot. Put practice sessions on the bottom axis and baskets made in a ten-shot challenge on the side axis.

Describe how the dots move

A scatterplot shows two numbers for each player. Pearson correlation, called r, describes the direction and strength of a straight-line relationship. It ranges from −1 to +1. An upward pattern is positive, a downward pattern is negative, and a value near zero means a weak straight-line pattern. A strong curve can have a correlation near zero, so look at the dots first.

Draw a line to make a prediction

Simple linear regression uses one predictor to estimate an outcome. Our example line predicts 2 baskets plus 1 for each practice session. The slope is the predicted change per extra session. The intercept is the prediction at zero sessions. A residual is the actual score minus the predicted score. Other factors, such as experience, could help explain the pattern. Keep predictions near the range of practice sessions you observed.

Try it

At 3 practice sessions, the line predicts 5 baskets. If a player makes 6, what is the residual?

Show answer

+1 basket, because the actual score is 6 and the predicted score is 5.

Go deeper with the math

The hat on y means a fitted value. Least squares chooses the line that minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals.

$$\hat y=b_0+b_1x,\quad e=y-\hat y$$

Chapter 10

Multiple Regression

A teacher wants to predict a reading quiz score using both practice sets finished and hours slept. How can we describe the link with practice when students sleep for different amounts of time?

Change one number at a time

Multiple regression uses two or more predictors. Imagine a model that predicts 40 points plus 2 for each practice set and 3 for each hour of sleep. With sleep fixed at 8 hours, going from 3 to 4 practice sets raises the prediction from 70 to 72. The coefficient 2 means the model predicts 2 more points per extra set holding sleep fixed. It describes a pattern in the model. A separate study design would help test whether practice causes the change.

Check whether the model travels well

Predictors sometimes overlap. If students who finish more sets almost always sleep longer, the model may struggle to separate their contributions. This is called multicollinearity. Check predictions on new data if your goal is prediction. Also look at the residuals to see whether errors show a curve or get bigger for certain predicted scores. Those patterns can signal that the model needs work.

Try it

For two predictions with the same number of practice sets, how much higher is the predicted score with 8 hours of sleep than with 7?

Show answer

3 points. The practice term stays fixed and the sleep term goes up by 3.

Go deeper with the math

Here x₁ and x₂ are the predictors. Each slope describes the fitted change for one additional unit of that predictor with the other held fixed. This additive example uses the same practice slope at every sleep value.

$$\hat y=b_0+b_1x_1+b_2x_2$$

Chapter 11

Categorical Data & Chi-Square

A school club surveys 40 students about their favorite game. Each chooses chess or soccer. Do the two grades show different preferences?

Put the counts in a table

In Grade 6, 14 pick chess and 6 pick soccer. In Grade 7, 6 pick chess and 14 pick soccer. These are the observed counts. If grade and preference were independent, each grade’s 20 students would split according to the overall 20–20 preference totals. That gives an expected count of 10 in each cell. A chi-square test of independence checks how far the observed table is from that expectation.

Put a number on the mismatch

For each cell, subtract expected from observed, square the gap, and divide by the expected count. Each cell here contributes 16/10 = 1.6, for a total of 6.4. This is the Pearson chi-square statistic. Larger totals are stronger evidence against independence when judged against the appropriate chi-square distribution. Use counts of independent observations with one response per person. Very small expected counts can make the usual approximation unreliable. A 2-by-2 table with small counts can use Fisher’s exact test.

Try it

If 12 of 40 students had chosen chess overall and Grade 6 still had 20 students, how many Grade 6 chess votes would you expect under independence?

Show answer

6. The overall chess share would be 12/40, so multiply that share by the 20 Grade 6 students.

Go deeper with the math

For a table with r rows and c columns, the degrees of freedom are (r − 1)(c − 1). In the formula, O is an observed count and E is its expected count under independence.

$$E=\frac{\text{row total}\times\text{column total}}{\text{grand total}},\quad \chi^2=\sum_{\mathrm{cells}}\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}$$

Chapter 12

Nonparametric Methods

Two groups rate a puzzle from 1 star to 5 stars. A 5-star rating ranks above 4 stars. The jump from 4 to 5 might feel different from the jump from 1 to 2. We can use the order of the ratings to compare groups.

Swap values for their positions

A rank is a position in a sorted list. Imagine Team A gives ratings 1, 2, 4 and Team B gives 3, 4, 5. The two 4s tie for positions 4 and 5, so each gets rank 4.5. Team A’s ranks sum to 7.5 and Team B’s sum to 13.5. The bigger rank sum for Team B suggests its ratings tend to be higher in this tiny example. A test helps judge the evidence with the sample size and ties in mind.

Choose a method that fits the design

The Mann–Whitney or Wilcoxon rank-sum test compares two independent groups using ranks. A difference in ranks can reflect differences in where scores sit or in their distribution shapes. The Kruskal–Wallis test extends a rank comparison to several independent groups. For matched measurements, a sign test can count which direction each pair changes, or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test can use signs and ranked change sizes when its assumptions fit.

Try it

If three values tie for ranks 2, 3, and 4, what rank does each get?

Show answer

Each gets rank 3, the average of 2, 3, and 4.

Go deeper with the math

For signed ranks, discard zero differences in the usual version, rank the absolute differences, and total the ranks attached to positive changes. The standard paired test assumes the differences are independent across pairs and symmetric about zero under the null. Rank tests still depend on the sampling or assignment design.

$$W_+=\sum_{i:\,d_i>0}\operatorname{rank}(|d_i|)$$

Chapter 13

Effect Size, Power & Putting It Together

Two paper-airplane designs differ by 20 centimeters on average. That might matter if the club’s record is only 10 centimeters away. If the goal is to fly an extra meter, the same difference may feel small.

How big is the difference?

An effect size describes the size of a difference or relationship. A raw difference of 20 centimeters is already an effect size. A standardized difference such as Cohen’s d gives the difference in standard deviation units. If the pooled standard deviation is 40 centimeters, d = 20/40 = 0.50. An interval gives a sense of precision, and your goal helps you judge whether the difference matters.

How likely is the study to spot it?

Power is the chance that a planned test will reject its null claim when a particular real effect exists. If a plan has 80% power for a 20-centimeter effect, about 80 out of 100 repeated studies would detect it if that effect and the planning assumptions hold. Larger samples generally raise power for the same effect and cutoff. To plan your own study, write a question first. Decide what size of effect would matter, choose how to collect and compare the data, and pick a sample size. Then report the effect you found, its interval, and how you ran the study.

Try it

The club randomly assigns each independently built plane to one of two designs and records one distance per plane. What would you analyze and report?

Show answer

Plot both groups, compare their mean distances using a method for independent groups such as Welch’s t-test, and report the difference in centimeters with its confidence interval. Explain whether the estimated gain matters for the club’s goal.

Go deeper with the math

For two independent groups, one common version of Cohen’s d divides the mean difference by the pooled sample standard deviation. Power equals one minus the chance of a missed detection, β, for the specified effect and study plan.

$$d=\frac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{s_{\mathrm{pooled}}},\quad \mathrm{power}=1-\beta$$

Read and clean the data

A CSV is a table written as text. Each row in this fictional reading log is one student’s record for one day. Trim extra spaces so “ Mon ” and “Mon” match. Remove the exact duplicate, then leave out rows that lack a measurement or have a negative count. A real zero is useful data, so keep it.

csv_text <- "student,day,minutes,pages
A, Mon ,20,12
A,Tue,30,18
B,Mon,15,9
B,Tue,25,10
B,Tue,25,10
C,Mon,10,0
C,Tue,40,24
D,Mon,0,0
D,Tue,20,8
E,Mon,,7
F,Tue,12,-2"
raw <- read.csv(text = csv_text, na.strings = c("", "NA"),
                stringsAsFactors = FALSE)

clean <- raw
clean$day <- trimws(clean$day)
clean <- unique(clean)
clean <- clean[complete.cases(clean) &
               clean$minutes >= 0 & clean$pages >= 0, ]

cat(nrow(raw), "raw rows;", nrow(clean), "clean rows\n")
print(clean)

Check your result. You should see 11 raw rows and 8 clean rows. Student D’s Monday record stays because both recorded values are zero.

Make a new column

A rate helps compare sessions of different lengths. Divide pages by minutes to find pages per minute. Calculate the rate only when reading time is greater than zero. A zero-minute record has no defined reading rate, while reading zero pages in ten minutes gives a rate of zero.

clean$pages_per_minute <- NA_real_

  clean$pages[has_time] / clean$minutes[has_time]

Make a new column

A rate helps compare sessions of different lengths. Divide pages by minutes to find pages per minute. Calculate the rate only when reading time is greater than zero. A zero-minute record has no defined reading rate, while reading zero pages in ten minutes gives a rate of zero.

has_time = clean["minutes"] > 0
clean["pages_per_minute"] = clean["pages"].div(
    clean["minutes"].where(has_time)
)

print(clean[["student", "day", "pages_per_minute"]])

Check your result. A’s Monday rate is 12 ÷ 20 = 0.60 pages per minute. D’s Monday rate is missing, shown as NA in R and NaN in pandas.

Filter and sort rows

Ask a smaller question. Which records show at least 20 minutes of reading? A filter keeps rows that match a rule. Sort the result so the longest sessions appear first. Keep this result in a new table so you can still use the full clean dataset later.

long_sessions = clean.loc[clean["minutes"] >= 20].sort_values(
    ["minutes", "student", "day"], ascending=[False, True, True]
)

print(long_sessions[["student", "day", "minutes", "pages"]])

Check your result. Five rows pass the filter. C’s 40-minute Tuesday session comes first.

Try it. Change the rule from at least 20 minutes to more than 20 minutes. How many rows remain?

Python · Chapter 4

Summarize by day

A group summary turns many rows into one row per group. Split the full clean table by day, count the records, and calculate the mean pages for each day. The mean is total pages divided by the number of records in that group, including the valid zero-page records.

daily = clean.groupby("day", as_index=False).agg(
    records=("pages", "size"),
    mean_pages=("pages", "mean"),
)

print(daily)

Check your result. Monday has four records and a mean of 5.25 pages. Tuesday has four records and a mean of 15 pages.

Try it. Calculate Monday’s mean by hand from the four page counts. What changes if you drop the two zero-page records?

Python · Chapter 5

Draw and read a bar chart

A bar chart compares categories. Here each bar stands for a day and its height shows mean pages per record. Start the vertical axis at zero so the bar heights show the comparison fairly. These two days describe the reading log. You would need more data to tell whether Tuesday is usually higher.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4))
bars = ax.bar(daily["day"], daily["mean_pages"], color="#17628b")
ax.set(title="Average pages by day", xlabel="Day",
       ylabel="Mean pages per record", ylim=(0, 18))
ax.bar_label(bars, padding=3)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Check your result. The Tuesday bar reaches 15 pages and the Monday bar reaches 5.25. The gap is 9.75 pages per record.

Try it. Make a second chart that shows the mean minutes by day. Do the two charts tell the same story about which day was higher?
